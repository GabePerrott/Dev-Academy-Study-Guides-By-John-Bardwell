Domain Driven Design · W1D3

You can't code what you don't understand yet

Domain Driven Design sounds grand, but the idea is plain: before you write a line, you have to actually know the thing you're modelling. Your computer doesn't know how bowling is scored. You have to learn it, then teach it.

So this guide does the domain first — with a scorecard you can play — and only then turns to code. That order isn't decoration; it's the entire lesson.

domain → data → pseudocode → code the pipeline this whole challenge runs on.
01

What DDD actually is

Taking knowledge that lives in a human head and encoding it as logic a machine can follow.

You might know a thing intuitively — how to score tennis, judge a snowboard run, tell a good coffee from a bad one. Your computer knows none of it. Domain Driven Design is the discipline of getting that specific knowledge out of your head and into your code, deliberately, before and during the build.

The "domain" here is bowling. And the reason it's a famously hard challenge isn't the JavaScript — it's that you need to understand bowling and the code at the same time, and gaps in either one look identical from the outside: a wrong number.

◆ Insight — why the wrong answer is actually useful

Because bowling has a knowable correct score, your code has a built-in truth check. If you get 64 and the answer is 68, you don't have a mysterious bug — you have a flaw in your understanding that has been made visible. That's the real gift of a domain challenge: the domain grades your logic for you. Chase the difference, and you'll find either a misread rule or a mistranslated one.

▲ Worth knowing — this is most of professional work

Almost every job you'll take is DDD in disguise: an insurance firm's claim rules, a school's timetable constraints, a shop's discount tiers. The code is rarely the hard part. Understanding the business well enough to encode it — and noticing where the experts' explanation is vague or contradictory — is the job. Bowling is a small, safe rehearsal for exactly that.

02

The domain: bowling

Learn the rules by playing, not by reading. Click pins and watch the bonuses resolve.

The basics: a game is 10 frames. In each frame you roll two balls to knock down 10 pins — unless you knock them all down with the first ball, which ends the frame early. Simple so far. The complexity is entirely in the bonuses.

Open frame
ball1 + ball2

Didn't clear all 10. Score is just the sum. No bonus.

Spare /
10 + next 1 ball

All 10 down using both balls. Bonus: your next ball.

Strike X
10 + next 2 balls

All 10 down on the first ball. Bonus: your next two balls.

10th frame
just add its balls

Strike or spare here earns a 3rd ball. Then simply sum them.

Now play. Click a pin count for each roll — the scorecard fills in live, and frames waiting on a bonus show a dash until the bonus balls arrive. That dash is the whole concept made visible.

✖ The thing that makes bowling hard to code

A frame's score can't be known when the frame ends. A strike in frame 4 stays unresolved until you've bowled frames 5 and 6. That's why the scorecard shows a dash — and it's why you can't simply loop once adding pairs of numbers. Your code has to look forward into later frames. Every difficult part of this challenge traces back to that single fact.

Which kind of frame is this?

Before you can apply a rule, your code has to identify which rule applies. Try some combinations:

Checkpoint: how many bonus balls does a spare earn?
▲ Clearing up the 10th frame confusion

This tripped up the room in the lecture, so plainly: in frames 1–9 a strike ends the frame — you gave up your second ball, and you get the next two balls as a bonus instead. In the 10th frame there is no "next frame" to borrow from, so they let you roll the bonus balls right there. Strike or spare in the 10th → you roll a 3rd ball, and the frame simply scores as the sum of its balls. Don't try to apply the bonus rules there; treat the last frame as its own special case.

03

Modelling it as data

Turning "10 frames of 2 balls" into a shape JavaScript can hold — an array of arrays.

Here's where domain meets code. The domain says: a game has 10 frames; a frame has 2 balls. That maps almost word-for-word onto a structure you already know from the nested data guide — a 2D array:

Code anatomy — the game, modelled
const frames = [ [3, 4], [10, 0], [5, 5], … ]
Hover or tap a highlighted token above · outer index = frame, inner index = ball
—
Every part explains its own job.

So frames[0] is the first frame (an array), and frames[0][1] is the second ball of the first frame (a number). Exactly the [row][col] pattern from the Dejarik board — outer index picks the frame, inner index picks the ball.

◆ Insight — good models make the code obvious

Notice how little translation was needed: "10 frames, each with 2 balls" became [[a,b], [a,b], …] almost mechanically. That's the sign of a good model. When you find yourself fighting your data shape — writing gnarly code to get at simple facts — it's usually the model that's wrong, not you. Changing the shape is often easier than out-coding a bad one.

Three frames so far. frames[1] is a whole frame (an array); frames[1][0] reaches into it for the first ball. The last line sums frame 2's balls to 10 — which is precisely how you'd detect a spare.
04

Design before code

Four questions and a habit of writing comments first. This is the part people skip, and it's the part that saves you.

Before writing any function, answer four questions. If you can answer all four, you'll usually write it right the first time — and when you don't, you'll know exactly where to look.

The function design checklist
1. What are the inputs?
What gets passed in, and in what shape? For scoreFrame: one frame — an array of two numbers.
2. What work does it do?
The one job, in a sentence. "Add the two balls together." If your sentence needs an "and", consider two functions.
3. What are the outputs?
What comes back, and of what type? A single number. Knowing this tells you what to return.
4. Any side effects?
Does it change anything outside itself, or log? Mostly you want none — pure in, pure out. (Straight from the mutability guide.)

Pseudocode first

Write your plan as comments, then fill in code beneath each one. It feels pedantic for about a minute, and then it starts catching your mistakes before they exist. Step through how a function grows:

        complicated things?" — mostly, they write the plan down first.

      

    


    
    

      
05
Top-down vs bottom-up

      
Two legitimate ways in. Same destination, different first move — and different failure modes.


      
The lecture built the same solution twice to show the contrast. Flip between them:


      

        

          
          
        

        

          
Start with the smallest thing you can get working — score a single frame — then build outward
          until it covers the game.

          
// 1. smallest piece first: score ONE frame
function scoreFrame(frame) {
  return frame[0] + frame[1]
}

// 2. then build outward, using the piece you trust
function totalScore(gameFrames) {
  // create a total
  // loop over the frames
  //   add the frame score to the total
  // return the total
}

          
Trade-off: you get working, testable functions fast, and each one is easy to
          verify on its own. The risk is assembly — you can end up with a pile of good pieces and a fiddly job
          fitting them together.

        

        

          
Start with the whole problem in one function, get an answer, then carve out smaller functions as
          it gets unwieldy.

          
// start with the whole job in one place
function totalScore(gameFrames) {
  let total = 0
  for (let i = 0; i < gameFrames.length; i++) {
    const currentFrame = gameFrames[i]
    total += currentFrame[0] + currentFrame[1]
  }
  return total
}
// later: pull that += line out into its own scoreFrame()

          
Trade-off: you reach a working answer sooner, which feels great. The risk is
          that it snowballs — by the time you add spares and strikes, one giant function becomes very hard to
          reason about. It demands disciplined refactoring as you go.

        

      


      

        
▲ My steer for this challenge specifically

        
Start bottom-up. Bowling's rules are naturally small and separable — isStrike,
        isSpare, scoreFrame — and small functions are far easier to check, which
        matters enormously when the domain itself is the tricky bit. If a number comes out wrong, you want to test
        one rule in isolation, not bisect a 40-line function. You can always work top-down for the outer loop and
        bottom-up for the rules; most real work mixes both.

      


      
Both versions run on the same data — here's the bottom-up pair, complete for a basic game. Run it, then
      try breaking it with a spare:


      

        
basic-game.js

        
const frames = [[1,4],[4,5],[6,3],[5,3],[2,6],[1,7],[3,4],[2,2],[3,3],[0,0]]

function scoreFrame(frame) {
  return frame[0] + frame[1]
}

function totalScore(gameFrames) {
  let total = 0
  gameFrames.forEach(frame => {
    total += scoreFrame(frame)
  })
  return total
}

console.log(totalScore(frames))   // 64 — a basic game, no bonuses

// now try this: swap one frame for a spare and re-run.
// the answer WON'T change... but it should. why?


        

      


      

        
✖ Try it — the moment the simple version breaks

        
Change a frame to [5,5] in the code above and re-run. The total shifts only by the raw pin
        count, because scoreFrame has no idea what a spare is — it just adds two numbers. That's the
        exact wall this challenge is built around. Everything from here is teaching your code to notice special
        frames and look ahead for their bonuses.

      

    


    
    

      
06
Debug & refactor

      
Two habits that matter more than cleverness: log to find out what's true, and clean up while it's still small.


      
The console.log habit

      
The lecturer hit a real bug live — a loop written as i < frames.length + 1, which ran 11
      times over a 10-item array and crashed. The fix wasn't cleverness. It was logging i inside the
      loop and seeing it count to 10 when it should have stopped at 9.


      

        
off-by-one.js

        
const frames = [[1,4],[4,5],[6,3]]

// the bug: "+ 1" makes it run one time too many
for (let i = 0; i < frames.length + 1; i++) {
  console.log('i is', i, '→ frame is', frames[i])
}
// watch the last line: undefined. Reading [0] off THAT is the crash.

        

      


      

        
◆ Insight — this is the undefined cliff again

        
The error you'd get is "Cannot read properties of undefined" — the same cliff from the nested
        data guide. It always means a step earlier than the error came back empty. Here, frames[3]
        doesn't exist, so reading [0] off it explodes. When you see that message, don't stare at the
        line that threw; ask "what just handed me nothing?"

      


      

        
▲ Guessing vs finding out

        
When something's wrong there are two moves: change things semi-randomly and re-run, or log a value and
        find out what's actually true. The first feels faster and almost never is. One
        console.log inside the loop turned a mystery crash into an obvious off-by-one in seconds.
        Print the thing you're assuming.

      


      
Refactor while it's small

      
Each time a piece works, stop and ask three questions before moving on:

      
    
        
  • Naming — would scoreFrame read better than calc? Will this make sense next week?
    • 
        
  • Simplify — is there duplication I can pull out?
    • 
        
  • Size — is this function doing too much? Should part of it become its own function?
    • 
      

      
Doing this during the build is far cheaper than at the end. Bowling gets genuinely complicated once
      strikes arrive, and messy code at that point compounds fast.


      

        
◆ Insight — the 15-minute rule

        
Stuck for more than 15 minutes? Ask. It's tempting to keep grinding combinations, but stuck-time rarely
        converts to learning — you're usually missing one fact, and a two-minute conversation supplies it. Getting
        unstuck fast means more of your afternoon is spent actually building. Asking early is a skill, not a
        concession.

      

    


    
    

      
✦
Your challenge

      
The bowling kata, staged from basic to full. Nudges unlock one at a time — no finished solutions.


      

        
How to attack this

        
Work the stages in order and get each one fully working before moving on: basic game → spares →
        strikes → last frame. Don't try to write the whole scorer at once; that's the fastest route to a tangle.

        
For the assessment you need iteration over the frames array and a conditional — which in
        practice means reaching at least the spares stage. Commit often (even just pseudocode), and if you're
        pairing, make sure you each have a couple of commits on the branch.

      


      

        
Stage 1 — basic game

        
// Score a game with no spares and no strikes.
// Target: the sample basic game totals 64.
function totalScore(gameFrames) {}

        

          
          
Plan
Write the pseudocode before code: create a total, loop over
          the frames, add each frame's score to the total, return the total. Those four comments are your
          function's skeleton.

          
          
The pieces
You need a running total that starts at
          0, a loop (forEach or a for loop — either is fine), and
          += to accumulate. A separate scoreFrame(frame) that returns
          frame[0] + frame[1] keeps it tidy and gives you something testable.

          
          
Watch for
Two classics: forgetting the return
          at the end (you'll get undefined), and declaring total inside the loop so it
          resets every iteration. Declare it before the loop. Test as you go with a console.log.

        

      


      

        
Stage 2 — detect the frame type

        
// Before you can score a special frame, you must spot one.
function isStrike(frame) {}
function isSpare(frame) {}

        

          
          
Concept
Each returns a boolean — true or false. Think about what
          is uniquely true of each. A strike: all ten pins on the first ball. A spare: ten pins total, but
          not on the first ball alone.

          
          
The tests
Strike → the first ball equals 10. Spare → the two
          balls sum to 10. Careful: a strike written [10, 0] also sums to 10, so your spare
          check must exclude strikes — either test isSpare only when it isn't a strike, or build that
          condition into the check itself.

          
          
Shape
Each is a one-line return of a
          comparison — no if statement needed, since a comparison already produces a boolean. Order
          matters when you use them: check strike first, then spare, then fall through to basic.

        

      


      

        
Stage 3 — spares

        
// A spare scores 10 + the NEXT BALL bowled.
// You'll need to look into the following frame.

        

          
          
Concept
Your per-frame scoring can no longer look at just one
          frame — it needs to see the next one. That means the function needs access to the whole array and
          the current position, not a lone frame. Rethink its inputs (checklist question 1).

          
          
The tools
Pass in frames and an index
          i. The next ball after frame i is the first ball of frame
          i + 1. A for loop hands you i naturally;
          forEach can too — its callback takes the index as a second argument.

          
          
Watch for
When a spare lands in the last frame there's no
          frames[i + 1] — that's the undefined cliff waiting. Handle the 10th frame separately (stage
          5) and this disappears. Test with a known case: [5,5] then [3,0] should score
          13 + 3 = 16.

        

      


      

        
Stage 4 — strikes

        
// A strike scores 10 + the NEXT TWO BALLS bowled.
// Careful: those two balls might span two frames.

        

          
          
Concept
Same idea as spares but two balls deep — and here's the
          trap: if the next frame is also a strike, it only contains one real ball. So your second bonus
          ball comes from the frame after that. Consecutive strikes are what makes this stage hard.

          
          
Two routes
A) Handle it with conditionals: if
          frames[i+1] is a strike, take its first ball plus the first ball of
          frames[i+2]; otherwise take both balls of frames[i+1]. B) Build a flat
          list of every ball bowled in order, then the bonus is simply the next two entries. Route B is more
          thinking up front but far less branching.

          
          
Verify
Test against known totals rather than eyeballing:
          a strike followed by [3,4] then all zeros = 24. All ten frames strikes (with the
          10th's bonus balls) = 300. If you hit 300, your strike logic is genuinely correct — it's the
          strictest test there is.

        

      


      

        
Stage 5 — the last frame

        
// The 10th frame is its own special case:
// a strike or spare there earns a 3rd ball, and
// the frame simply scores as the sum of its balls.

        

          
          
Concept
Don't try to make the general rules stretch to cover
          frame 10 — that way lies pain. Treat it as an exception: no lookahead, no bonus logic, just add up
          whatever balls it holds (two or three).

          
          
Shape
A conditional on the index does it — when
          i is the last frame, sum its balls and skip the bonus branches entirely. Summing an array of
          unknown length is a reduce, or a small loop. This also quietly fixes the stage-3 and stage-4
          cliff, since the last frame never looks forward.

        

      


      

        
◆ Test targets to check yourself against

        
Rather than squinting at numbers, verify against known-correct games. All gutter balls → 0. All
        ones → 20. The sample basic game → 64. Every frame a 5/5 spare (with a final 5) →
        150. All strikes → 300. Get those five and your scorer is right. The scorecard in section 2
        will reproduce any of them if you want to see the frame-by-frame breakdown.
